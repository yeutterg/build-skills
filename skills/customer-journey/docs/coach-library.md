### Principles by interview phase

#### Company and goals

- Pick a mission and what you want to learn, not the money. Ask: "If money were equal everywhere, why this problem?" [Ferriss #403] [Lex #294]
- Great ideas chase you: try to run away; if it keeps pulling you back after weeks of research, commit. Ask: "How long has this problem refused to leave you alone?" [Lex #294] [Build]
- A good idea solves a real, recurring problem and has a clear why. Ask: "What is the one-sentence why behind this company?" [Build]
- Focus on the one best idea and fold the others into it. Ask: "Which goal would you keep if you could only keep one?" [Lex #294]
- Be slightly early, not too early or late, so you educate the market and lead it. Ask: "What changed that makes this possible now?" [Design Better]

#### Products and generations (v1/v2/v3)

- v1 must be disruptive but still understandable (the Segway failed partly because people didn't get it). Ask: "What exactly is disruptive in v1, and can a stranger grasp it in one sentence?" [Build]
- v1 has hard limits: small team and budget, limited features, 9 to 18 months, a firm deadline. Expect bad unit economics and high acquisition cost; the job is proving early adopters will buy. Ask: "What did you leave out on purpose?" [Build]
- v2 fixes what's wrong without overturning what customers know, using real customer data. Ask: "Is v2 fixing what v1 buyers complain about, or chasing something new?" [Build]
- Get to v2 fast; added features should fill gaps in the customer journey to reach a wider audience. [Build]
- v3 optimizes the business: margins, suppliers, sales, support, marketing efficiency, net profit. [Build]
- The iPod only took off in its third generation. Ask: "Which generation are you on, and are you judging it by the right yardstick?" [Ferriss #403]
- If a v1 fails, work out whether your gut or outside conditions were wrong. [Build]

#### Customers and the pain

- Start from a pain you can see and feel; if you feel it, others likely do, which hints at market size. Ask: "Where have you personally felt this?" [Design Better]
- Painkiller, not vitamin. Ask: "If this vanished tomorrow, what would customers go back to suffering through?" [Lex #294]
- Aim past painkiller to a superpower for ordinary people, not geeks. Ask: "What can customers suddenly do that they couldn't before?" [Design Better]
- See the invisible problem: people habituate to annoyances until they stop noticing. Ask: "What do customers put up with that they no longer complain about?" [Build] [Ferriss #403]
- Target precisely: General Magic aimed at average consumers when tech-savvy business users were the realistic buyers. Ask: "Who is the first customer who will truly care?" [Build]
- Paying customers give the most reliable insight. Ask: "Is that from paying customers or from people you pitched?" [Build]

#### Market, positioning and category

- You can disrupt more than one thing: Nest changed the product and the channel (direct to consumer). Ask: "Are you disrupting the product, the channel, the model, or several?" [Build]
- In a category nobody chooses, first wake people up to having a choice. Ask: "Do buyers even know they have a choice here?" [Lex #294]
- Find who controls the channel: thermostat installers were rewarded for pushing certain brands, with no price transparency. Ask: "Who stands between you and the customer, and what's in it for them?" [Lex #294]
- A new category may have no shelf. Philips smartphones were acclaimed but stores had no aisle for them; for Nest he insisted on a connected home aisle. Ask: "Where does the customer find you, and does that place exist yet?" [Build]
- Being early lets you define the category so followers are measured against you. [Design Better]

#### Customer journey and touchpoints

- You sell a product experience, from discovery to installation to customer service, not just a product. Ask: "Walk me through the customer's first hour after the box arrives." [Build]
- Map the whole story with the team, especially marketing, including installation and support. Build has a customer journey and touchpoints map for this. [Build]
- Out-of-box experience matters: manning a support line as a teenager taught him to fix installation pain at the source. Ask: "What do support tickets say about first use?" [Lex #294]
- Remove setup chores: batteries shipped charged instead of "charge before use"; a Nest screw that fits any wall. Ask: "What must the customer do before it works, and could you do it for them?" [Build]
- Packaging and website words are design too; move between the tiny detail and the overall feel. [Lex #294]
- Even required warnings can be designed: the Nest Cam's mandatory cord warning tag was made easy to peel off. [Bookworm #183]

#### Messaging and storytelling

- The story defines the product rather than selling it, so write it before building. Ask: "Could this message have been written before the product, and did the product follow it?" [Ferriss #590] [Build]
- Three parts: remind people of the annoyance, give logical and emotional reasons yours is best, simplify the tech so customers can retell it. Ask: "Can a customer explain this to a friend in one breath?" [Build]
- Plant the virus of doubt: take people back to the pain they stopped noticing. Ask: "Which line makes customers feel the pain again?" [Build] [Lex #294]
- Cover rational (money, time) and emotional (that's cool, I want it) reasons, for the individual, family and society. Ask: "What's the rational reason to buy, and the emotional one?" [Ferriss #590]
- Why before what; specs and feature lists come second. Ask: "Where does your messaging lead with specs?" [Lex #294]
- Bold beats cautious; cautious comms end up duller than the product, and boldness flows from the leader. [Lex #294]
- Keep refining the story by watching faces and testing it on trusted outsiders. [Lex #294] [Ferriss #590]
- "Three things in one" worked for the iPhone because each part was a real pain people carried. [Lex #294]
- Speak other people's languages: marketing, finance, business. [Ferriss #403]

#### Decisions: opinion vs data (fact vs assumption)

- Name the decision type: data-driven or opinion-driven, which carries more risk. Ask: "Is that based on data or opinion? What's the data?" [Build] [Lex #294]
- Consumer v1s have little data, so most calls are opinions; B2B can get some by talking to customers. Ask: "Which of these beliefs could you test before launch?" [Lex #294] [Build]
- When it's opinion, explain the why and the trade-offs so the team owns it. [Lex #294]
- To defend an opinion, show you have all available data, know the customer, see the risks, and it serves the company. [Build]
- Data can overturn opinion: no SIM slot reversed after carrier data; the Mac-only iPod gained Windows after market share data. Ask: "What evidence would change your mind?" [Lex #294] [Build]
- Don't dress opinions up as data to dodge blame, as paralyzed companies and consultants do. [Lex #294]
- Opinions become data by doing: act, learn, form the next opinion. [Lex #294]

#### Teams and ownership of messaging (product management)

- Marketing is many disciplines (PR, social, creative, activation); product management or product marketing is the voice of the customer. Ask: "Who on your team is the voice of the customer?" [Lex #294]
- Product management writes the deep messages (for example, "save 20% of energy"); marketing turns them into creative. Ask: "Who owns your core messages, and who turns them into ads?" [Lex #294]
- Product management keeps everyone tied to the press release so marketing doesn't add adjectives the product can't deliver. Ask: "Which claims couldn't the product back up today?" [Lex #294]
- Early on, the product manager is usually the founder. [Lex #294]
- Great product managers learn what customers want, tell the builders, then turn it into marketing messages. [Build]
- Commission-only salespeople may mislead customers; vested commission keeps incentives honest. [Build]
- Critique the work, never the person. [Design Better] [Ferriss #590]

#### Launches and press releases

- Write the press release first, as in Amazon's working backwards; he did this for Build itself. Ask: "Show me the press release. What's the headline?" [Build] [Ferriss #590]
- Keep it short and newsworthy: why customers will buy, and the step change over what exists. Ask: "What's the step change, in the customer's words?" [Build]
- The product is ready when it mostly matches the press release; if you've pivoted, write a new one. [Build]
- Plan go-to-market as hard as the product: he joined the iPod only after Jobs committed at least two quarters of marketing dollars to it. Ask: "What's your launch budget and who is it aimed at?" [Lex #294]
- Release on a predictable rhythm: about one big launch and a few smaller updates a year. [Build]

### Stories he tells

- **CDs in the car.** A DJ with a thousand CDs who never had the right one; compressed audio plus a pocket hard drive became the iPod. Use when: a founder can't name a pain they've felt. [Lex #294] [Design Better]
- **Fruit stickers.** We stop seeing barcode stickers on fruit or "charge before use" labels; Apple shipped devices charged. Use when: a founder says customers "don't really complain." [Build]
- **General Magic.** A proto-iPhone 15 years early, built around what the team found cool, with no clear target or reason to buy. Use when: features are described without a customer. [Ferriss #403] [Ferriss #590]
- **The missing aisle.** Philips devices failed in stores with no aisle for them; for Nest he insisted on a connected home aisle. Use when: discussing channel or a new category. [Build]
- **The cold vacation house.** He couldn't warm his Lake Tahoe house remotely, so trips began cold; that led to Nest. Use when: exploring an origin story. [Build]
- **The thermostat nobody chose.** Installers chose the model and priced by the look of the house. Use when: positioning in a sleepy, channel-controlled category. [Lex #294]
- **Styrofoam iPod.** A foam model helped win Jobs's green light. Use when: a founder sells with words instead of something to hold. [Build]
- **No SIM slot.** Jobs's no-slots rule fell to carrier data days later. Use when: a strong opinion needs facts. [Lex #294]
- **Plastic to glass.** Early iPhone plastic screens scratched; Jobs called it a design problem the team owned. Use when: a founder blames customers for a complaint. [Lex #294]
- **Three things in one.** The laziest marketing line worked because every piece named a real pain. Use when: a message sounds generic. [Lex #294]
- **Nest Cam warning tag.** A mandatory warning had to ship on the cord, so they made it easy to remove. Use when: legal copy affects a touchpoint. [Bookworm #183]
