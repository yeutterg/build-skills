## Forcing questions

Ask one at a time. Push until the answer is specific, evidenced and a little uncomfortable. Adapted from gstack's office hours (MIT, Copyright (c) 2026 Garry Tan); examples are fictional.

### 1. Demand reality

**Ask:** What is the strongest evidence that someone actually wants this? Not interest, not a waitlist: who would be genuinely upset if it disappeared tomorrow?

- **Good answers:** someone paid, came back, built a habit around it, complained loudly when it broke, told a friend unprompted.
- **Red flags:** "people say it's cool", signups, likes, investor excitement.

### 2. Status quo

**Ask:** What do people do today to solve this, even badly? What does that cost them in time, money or how they feel?

- **Good answers:** a specific workaround and its cost (three alarms and a groggy hour; a stovetop kettle and a thermometer).
- **Red flags:** "Nothing, that's why the opportunity is huge." If nobody does anything, the pain may not be strong enough to act on.

### 3. Name the human

**Ask:** Name the person who needs this most. Who are they, what does their day look like, and what happens to them if the problem stays unsolved?

- **Good answers:** a real person, ideally someone the founder has spoken to, with a concrete consequence. For consumer products the consequence is a daily pain or a social moment; for B2B it is a career outcome.
- **Red flags:** categories ("busy professionals", "health-conscious millennials"). You cannot email a category.

### 4. Narrowest wedge

**Ask:** What is the smallest version of this someone would pay for this week, not after the platform is finished?

- **Good answers:** one feature, one use, one segment, something that could ship or be sold in days.
- **Red flags:** "It only works once the whole ecosystem exists." Usually the value is not clear yet, not that the product is too small.
- **Bonus:** What if the customer had to do nothing at all to get the value? No setup, no app, no account.

### 5. Observation and surprise

**Ask:** Have you watched someone use it without helping them? What did they do that surprised you?

- **Good answers:** a specific surprise that contradicted an assumption. Customers using it in a way it was not designed for are often showing the real product.
- **Red flags:** surveys, demos, "it's going as expected". Surveys flatter, demos are theater, and "as expected" means not looking.

### 6. Future-fit

**Ask:** In three years the world will look different. Does this become more essential or less, and why?

- **Good answers:** a specific claim about how the customer's world changes and why that favors this product in particular.
- **Red flags:** market growth rates, "the technology keeps improving". Every competitor can say that.

### After the first answer

Check, briefly, before going on:

1. **Defined words.** "Seamless", "better", "premium", "natural": what would you measure?
2. **Hidden assumptions.** Name one thing the framing takes for granted and ask whether it is verified.
3. **Real or hypothetical.** "I think customers would..." is hypothetical. "Three owners told me..." is real (and still an anecdote).

If the framing is off, restate it constructively ("Let me try: you are really building X for Y. Closer?") and continue.

### Pushback patterns

| Founder says | Soft (avoid) | Forcing (aim for) |
|---|---|---|
| "Everyone who tries it loves it." | "Great, who have you talked to?" | "Loving it is free. Who paid, who came back, who got upset when it broke? Name one." |
| "Our market is growing 20% a year." | "Nice tailwind. How will you capture it?" | "Every competitor can quote that number. What do you believe about this market that makes your product more necessary?" |
| "We need the full platform first." | "What would a smaller version look like?" | "That's a warning sign. What one thing would someone pay for this week?" |
| "We want onboarding to feel seamless." | "What does onboarding look like today?" | "Seamless is a feeling, not a step. Which step loses people, how many, and have you watched it happen?" |
| "Our customers are design-minded professionals." | "Tell me more about them." | "That's a filter. Name one customer. What did they use before, and what made them switch?" |

### Common failure patterns to name out loud

- A solution looking for a problem
- Hypothetical customers
- Treating interest as demand
- Waiting to launch until it is perfect
- Competing with other startups while ignoring the status quo
- A metric that cannot fail (vanity numbers)
